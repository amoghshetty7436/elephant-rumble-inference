## 1. Fixed-recall / fixed-false-alarm operating points (Neyman-Pearson framing)

### Takeaway
Fixing one error rate and optimising the other is the formal Neyman-Pearson (NP) paradigm. It is the standard way to report detectors in speaker verification (DCF and DET operating points), keyword spotting (miss rate at a fixed number of false alarms per hour), and medical screening (specificity at a target sensitivity). A key statistical caution applies: setting the threshold so the *empirical* error equals the target on a finite validation set breaks the target about half the time. High-probability methods such as the NP umbrella algorithm correct for this.

### Cited Findings
- NP classification minimises type II error subject to type I error <= alpha. It is motivated by settings such as disease diagnosis and spam detection, where one error type is much more costly. [V] — [Tong, Feng & Li 2018, "Neyman-Pearson classification algorithms and NP receiver operating characteristics", *Science Advances* 4(2): eaao1659, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aao1659](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03109)
- Tong et al. show that naively thresholding so the *empirical* type I error is <= alpha "does not satisfy the type I error control objective". The true error of such classifiers often exceeds alpha well. Their "umbrella algorithm" works with any scoring classifier (logistic regression, SVM, random forest): it chooses a threshold rank from a held-out order statistic so that P(type I error > alpha) <= delta. NP-ROC bands help choose alpha and compare classifiers, and an R package `nproc` implements both. [V for the naive-threshold failure and the umbrella/NP-ROC claims; the delta guarantee is K from the paper] — [Tong et al. 2018 (arXiv:1608.03109)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03109); [nproc CRAN](https://www.vps.fmvz.usp.br:443/CRAN/web/packages/nproc/index.html)
- Earlier formal treatments of NP learning include [K] [Scott & Nowak 2005, "A Neyman-Pearson approach to statistical learning", *IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory* 51(11):3806–3819, doi:10.1109/TIT.2005.856955](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.2005.856955) and [K] [Tong 2013, "A plug-in approach to Neyman-Pearson classification", *JMLR* 14:3011–3040](https://jmlr.org/papers/v14/tong13a.html).
- Recall-first applications usually swap the roles: false negatives (misses) are the controlled error. NP theory is symmetric, so one can cap the miss rate (recall >= 1 - alpha) and minimise the false-positive rate. This is an inference from the framework's symmetry. Tong et al. frame the type I error as the more severe one. — [Tong et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03109)
- **Speaker recognition (NIST SRE):** the primary metric is a normalised detection cost C_norm(theta) = P_miss(theta) + beta * P_fa(theta), with beta = (C_fa/C_miss)(1 - P_target)/P_target. SRE21 used C_miss = C_fa = 1 and P_target = 0.01 and 0.05, so the cost is averaged over two operating points on the DET curve. Systems must output calibrated log-likelihood ratios. The "actual" cost applies the Bayes threshold log(beta), while the "minimum" cost uses the oracle-best single threshold. The gap between actual and minimum DCF therefore measures miscalibration or threshold-choice error. [V] — [Sadjadi et al., "The 2021 NIST Speaker Recognition Evaluation", arXiv:2204.10242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10242); see also [NIST SRE16 eval plan](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2016/10/07/sre16_eval_plan_v1.3.pdf), [NIST SRE18 eval plan](https://www.nist.gov/document/sre18evalplan2018-05-31v6pdf)
- **Keyword spotting / wake-word detection:** the standard operating point is the false rejection rate (miss rate) at a fixed number of false alarms per hour (FAH), measured on long negative audio, and DET curves are used to compare across thresholds. Coucke et al. (Snips, ICASSP 2019) report FRR at 0.5 FAH. Their negative set has about 96 h of audio (dev: 20,321 negatives; test: 20,821 negatives), thresholds are tuned on dev, and results are reported on test. FRR was 0.12% clean and 1.60% noisy for the WaveNet model, versus 2.09% and 11.21% for an LSTM baseline. [V] — [Coucke et al. 2019, "Efficient keyword spotting using dilated convolutions and gating", arXiv:1811.07684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.07684). A survey of the field: [López-Espejo et al. 2022, "Deep Spoken Keyword Spotting: An Overview", *IEEE Access*, arXiv:2111.10592](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.10592) [K content]
- **DET curve:** this curve plots miss probability against false-alarm probability on normal-deviate (probit) axes. Curves for roughly Gaussian score distributions then become near-linear, which makes the low-error region easy to read. [K] — [Martin, Doddington, Kamm, Ordowski & Przybocki 1997, "The DET curve in assessment of detection task performance", *Proc. Eurospeech*, pp. 1895–1898](https://www.isca-archive.org/eurospeech_1997/martin97_eurospeech.html)
- **Medical screening:** reporting "specificity at sensitivity >= X%" (or the reverse) is common practice, and partial AUC was designed for this purpose (see Section 3). [K] — [McClish 1989, doi:10.1177/0272989X8900900307](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X8900900307)

### Inferences
- For a recall-first detector, the most decision-relevant headline number is "false positives per hour (or per unit reviewed) at recall >= R*". Reporting "precision at recall R*" is equivalent once prevalence is fixed. This mirrors KWS's "FRR at fixed FAH" with the roles of the two errors swapped.
- Calibrate the threshold so the recall target holds with high probability, not only on average. Use an order-statistic or binomial lower confidence bound on validation recall, which is the Tong et al. logic applied to positives. This matters most when there are few positives: with n = 50 validation positives, empirical recall of 0.90 is consistent with a true recall well below 0.90.
- The actual-vs-minimum DCF distinction suggests a transferable practice: report both performance at the threshold *chosen in advance* and the oracle best on test. The gap quantifies how much the threshold-selection procedure costs.

- I did not fetch a specific NIST OpenKWS / ATWV evaluation plan. ATWV (Actual Term-Weighted Value) applies a miss/false-alarm weighting with beta = 999.9 in OpenKWS, but this detail comes from prior knowledge and was not verified here.
- I did not verify a specific clinical guideline that sets a numeric sensitivity target, for example for cancer screening.

## 2. F-beta scores (F2): origin, criticisms, choice of beta

### Takeaway
F_beta (from van Rijsbergen's effectiveness measure) weights recall beta times as much as precision, so F2 is the usual "recall-heavy" single number. It is widely criticised. It ignores true negatives. Its implied precision/recall trade-off depends on the classifier rather than on the problem (Hand & Christen). Its optimal threshold behaves oddly: for a calibrated model it is F*/2 for F1, and at low prevalence an uninformative classifier "optimally" predicts everything positive. Beta is usually set by convention (beta = 2) rather than derived from costs.

### Cited Findings
- F_beta = (1 + beta^2) P R / (beta^2 P + R). It derives from van Rijsbergen's effectiveness measure E = 1 - F, where beta encodes that the user "attaches beta times as much importance to recall as precision". [K] — [van Rijsbergen 1979, *Information Retrieval*, 2nd ed., Butterworths, ch. 7 (online)](http://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/Keith/Preface.html); historical discussion in [Sasaki 2007, "The truth of the F-measure"](https://www.toyota-ti.ac.jp/Lab/Denshi/COIN/people/yutaka.sasaki/F-measure-YS-26Oct07.pdf) [K]
- Hand & Christen argue that the F-measure has a conceptual weakness. Written as a weighted arithmetic mean of precision and recall, its weights depend on the classifier or linkage method being evaluated, whereas "the relative importance assigned to precision and recall should be an aspect of the problem". Comparing methods by F therefore effectively uses different trade-offs for each method. They propose alternatives. [V] — [Hand & Christen 2018, "A note on using the F-measure for evaluating record linkage algorithms", *Statistics and Computing* 28:539–547, doi:10.1007/s11222-017-9746-6](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/e3bc0e58-14c1-43a5-b1fa-78b3fdbdcd10)
- Follow-up: F* = F/(2 - F) = TP/(TP + FP + FN), which is the Jaccard/critical success index, is offered as a more interpretable monotone transform. [K] — [Hand, Christen & Kirielle 2021, "F*: an interpretable transformation of the F-measure", *Machine Learning* 110:451–456, doi:10.1007/s10994-021-05964-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-021-05964-1)
- **Optimal threshold for F1:** for a calibrated classifier, "the optimal threshold ... is half the maximum F1". If the classifier is uninformative, F1 is maximised by predicting all examples positive. "Since the actual prevalence of positive examples typically is low, this behavior can be considered undesirable." For rare labels, a MeSH case study found terms such as "Platypus" predicted for over a third of documents. The authors state the results generalise to F_beta. [V] — [Lipton, Elkan & Narayanaswamy 2014, "Optimal thresholding of classifiers to maximize F1 measure", ECML PKDD, doi:10.1007/978-3-662-44851-9_15; arXiv:1402.1892](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.1892)
- How beta is chosen: in practice it is mostly convention (F2 for recall-heavy, F0.5 for precision-heavy). The Lipton et al. result implies the F_beta-optimal threshold for a calibrated model is F*_beta/(1 + beta^2). This is derived from their F1 result plus the stated generalisation, and was not shown explicitly in the text I read. [inference from V source]

### Inferences
- F2 is a reasonable *secondary* summary for a recall-first detector. As a model-selection criterion it can choose different implicit trade-offs for different models, and it can reward degenerate "flag everything" behaviour when positives are rare. Pairing it with a fixed-recall operating point, such as FP/hour at recall >= R*, removes the ambiguity.
- If the reviewing burden can be priced, for example minutes of manual review per false alarm versus the cost of a missed event, an explicit expected-cost criterion (Section 5) is more defensible than choosing beta.

### Gaps
- I found no principled published mapping from a misclassification cost ratio to beta. Some authors note that F_beta does not correspond to any linear cost. Treat this as an open point unless the writer has a source.

## 3. PR curves, average precision, partial AUC; PR vs ROC under imbalance

### Takeaway
ROC curves are prevalence-invariant. PR curves show how precision degrades at low prevalence and are favoured for imbalanced detection (Davis & Goadrich; Saito & Rehmsmeier). A 2024 NeurIPS paper (McDermott et al.) argues that "AUPRC is better under imbalance" is overgeneralised: AUPRC prioritises fixing high-score errors and can favour high-prevalence subgroups. For recall-first use, a *restricted-range* summary (partial AUC over the high-sensitivity region, or PR/ROC limited to recall >= R*) matches the decision better than full AUC or AP.

### Cited Findings
- A one-to-one correspondence exists between ROC and PR space for a given dataset: a curve dominates in ROC space iff it dominates in PR space. Linear interpolation is invalid in PR space, and optimising ROC-AUC does not optimise PR-AUC. [K] — [Davis & Goadrich 2006, "The relationship between precision-recall and ROC curves", ICML, doi:10.1145/1143844.1143874](https://doi.org/10.1145/1143844.1143874)
- Saito & Rehmsmeier find that the PR plot is "more informative than the ROC plot when evaluating binary classifiers on imbalanced datasets", because ROC can look optimistic when negatives vastly outnumber positives. [V title/venue; content K] — [Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015, *PLoS ONE* 10(3): e0118432, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0118432](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4349800/)
- Counterpoint: McDermott et al. show that "AUPRC is not generally superior in cases of class imbalance". AUROC weights all false positives equally, while AUPRC weights them by the inverse of the model's firing rate, so it favours fixing mistakes at high scores. AUPRC can also favour higher-prevalence subgroups (Spearman rho = 0.905 between the effect and the prevalence gap across fairness datasets). They recommend AUROC for context-independent comparison and settings "where false negatives are costly". They recommend AUPRC for high-cost, single-group, top-of-ranking intervention or information retrieval. Their literature review of more than 1.5M papers found the "AUPRC better under imbalance" claim is often uncited or misattributed. [V] — [McDermott, Zhang, Hansen, Angelotti & Gallifant 2024, "A closer look at AUROC and AUPRC under class imbalance", NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2401.06091](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.06091v4); [NeurIPS listing](https://mlanthology.org/neurips/2024/mcdermott2024neurips-closer)
- Partial AUC (pAUC): the area under the ROC curve over a restricted FPR (or TPR) range, proposed for focusing on clinically relevant regions, with a standardisation so random = 0.5. [K] — [McClish 1989, "Analyzing a portion of the ROC curve", *Medical Decision Making* 9(3):190–195, doi:10.1177/0272989X8900900307](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X8900900307)
- Nonparametric estimation and regression modelling of pAUC (covariate effects) are treated in [K] [Dodd & Pepe 2003, "Partial AUC estimation and regression", *Biometrics* 59(3):614–623, doi:10.1111/1541-0420.00071](https://doi.org/10.1111/1541-0420.00071).
- AP / AUPRC estimators differ (trapezoidal, interpolated, AP step-sum), and their confidence intervals behave differently. [K] — [Boyd, Eng & Page 2013, "Area under the precision-recall curve: point estimates and confidence intervals", ECML PKDD, doi:10.1007/978-3-642-40994-3_29](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-40994-3_29). PR-Gain curves address PR's non-linearity and the lack of a universal baseline. [K] — [Flach & Kull 2015, "Precision-Recall-Gain curves: PR analysis done right", NeurIPS](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/5867-precision-recall-gain-curves-pr-analysis-done-right)

### Inferences
- For a recall-first detector, the most aligned threshold-free summary is the *high-recall* partial area: pAUC over TPR in [R*, 1], or precision/FPR integrated over recall >= R*. Full AP is dominated by the high-precision, low-recall head of the ranking, which is the part a recall-first application cares about least. This is consistent with McDermott et al.'s point that AUPRC emphasises high-score errors.
- Precision depends on prevalence, so precision values from a validation set with a different positive rate than deployment will not transfer. FPR and false alarms per hour do transfer.

### Gaps
- I did not verify a standard published "high-recall pAUC" (TPR-restricted) variant beyond McClish/Dodd & Pepe, which focus mainly on FPR-restricted ranges. TPR-restricted pAUC appears in the literature (e.g. "two-way pAUC"), but I did not source it here.

## 4. DET curves, false alarms per hour, and DCASE sound event detection evaluation (PSDS)

### Takeaway
DCASE sound event detection moved from single-threshold event-based F1 (collar-based) to PSDS. PSDS integrates a polyphonic ROC curve (TPR vs. effective false positives per hour) up to a maximum false-alarm rate e_max. It uses intersection-based rather than collar-based matching and can penalise cross-class triggers and per-class instability. Threshold-independent PSDS computation (Ebbers et al.) removed the bias from evaluating only about 50 thresholds and showed how much threshold-dependent F1 can be inflated by threshold tuning.

### Cited Findings
- Segment-based and event-based (collar) metrics for polyphonic SED, including the common 200 ms onset collar and offset collar of max(200 ms, 20% of duration) used in DCASE, are defined in the `sed_eval` paper. [K] — [Mesaros, Heittola & Virtanen 2016, "Metrics for polyphonic sound event detection", *Applied Sciences* 6(6):162, doi:10.3390/app6060162](https://doi.org/10.3390/app6060162)
- PSDS (Bilen et al., ICASSP 2020) reduces per-class ROC curves into one polyphonic PSD-ROC and takes its normalised area. Matching uses intersection criteria: rho_DTC is the fraction of a detection that must overlap ground truth, otherwise it is a false positive. rho_GTC is the fraction of a ground-truth event that must be covered to count as a true positive. rho_CTTC is the overlap with another class's ground truth needed to count a false positive as a cross-trigger. eFPR = FP rate per unit time + alpha_CT * cross-trigger rate. The effective TPR = mean over classes minus alpha_ST * standard deviation across classes. e_max is "the maximum eFPR value of interest for the SED application", and the area is normalised by it. Collars are criticised because "collars introduce a limitation which could be hindering system evaluation": an ambiguous bark sequence can legitimately be labelled as one event or several. Single-operating-point F1 conflates modelling quality with threshold choice and can re-rank systems. In their Table 1, collar-F1 and PSDS rank three DCASE 2019 systems differently (PSDS 0.573 / 0.486 / 0.493 with alpha_CT = alpha_ST = 0, e_max = 100). [V] — [Bilen, Ferroni, Tuveri, Azcarreta & Krstulovic 2020, "A framework for the robust evaluation of sound event detection", ICASSP 2020, doi:10.1109/ICASSP40776.2020.9052995; arXiv:1910.08440](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.08440v2)
- DCASE 2024 Task 4 uses PSDS1 with rho_DTC = rho_GTC = 0.7, alpha_ST = 1.0, and e_max = 100 FPs per hour ("the maximum FP-rate e_max up to which the area under curve is computed"). Cross-trigger parameters were not used that year. MAESTRO data was instead scored with segment-based mean pAUC (segMPAUC) with a maximum FP rate of 0.1. The baseline post-processes with class-specific median filters tuned separately. [V] — [Cornell et al. 2024, "DCASE 2024 Task 4: Sound event detection with heterogeneous data and missing labels", arXiv:2406.08056](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08056)
- Threshold-independent PSDS: the DCASE 2021 evaluation approximated the PSD-ROC with 50 linearly spaced thresholds, which "can significantly underestimate PSDS". In their example, 50 thresholds gave 0.358 against an exact value of 0.400, and 500 thresholds gave 0.389. An exact method sorts all scores and accumulates per-threshold changes in TP/FP/cross-trigger counts. The authors recommend selecting operating points from exact PR curves on a *validation* set. Tuning per-class thresholds on validation raised collar-based F1 from 51.8% (threshold 0.5) to 57.2% on evaluation, which shows how much threshold-dependent metrics reflect operating-point tuning. [V] — [Ebbers, Haeb-Umbach & Serizel 2022, "Threshold independent evaluation of sound event detection scores", ICASSP 2022; arXiv:2201.13148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.13148); implementation: [sed_scores_eval (GitHub)](https://github.com/fgnt/sed_scores_eval) [K]
- Additional discussion of SED metric choices: [Ferroni et al. 2021, "Improving sound event detection metrics: insights from DCASE 2020", ICASSP, arXiv:2010.13648](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.13648) [K content]

### Inferences
- For a recall-first detector on long recordings, PSDS-style evaluation fits directly. Use TPR against false positives per hour, and choose e_max to cover the false-alarm budget reviewers can tolerate. A complementary view is "recall at X FP/hour" or "FP/hour at recall R*". Intersection-based matching (rho_DTC/rho_GTC) suits events with fuzzy boundaries better than tight collars do.
- PSDS integrates over all operating points up to e_max, so it is a model-selection metric. The deployed threshold should still be chosen on validation and reported with test performance at that pre-chosen threshold.

## 5. Cost-sensitive threshold selection (expected cost of misses vs. manual review)

### Takeaway
If the cost of a missed detection (C_FN) and the cost of a false alarm (C_FP, e.g. manual review time) can be stated, decision theory gives the Bayes-optimal threshold on calibrated probabilities: predict positive when p >= C_FP/(C_FP + C_FN). Elkan recommends training on the data as given and then thresholding calibrated probability estimates, rather than rebalancing the training data. The NIST DCF is exactly this framework with priors made explicit.

### Cited Findings
- Elkan (IJCAI 2001, pp. 973–978) characterises "reasonable" cost matrices and proves how to change the proportion of negative training examples so a cost-insensitive learner makes cost-optimal decisions. He argues that rebalancing has little effect for Bayesian and decision-tree learners. He recommends training on the data as given and then using the classifier's probability estimates to choose the decision with lowest expected cost. With correct probabilities this yields the threshold p* = c(1,0)/(c(1,0) + c(0,1)), i.e. FP cost over FP + FN cost. [V abstract; formula K] — [Elkan 2001, "The foundations of cost-sensitive learning", IJCAI](https://mlanthology.org/ijcai/2001/elkan2001ijcai-foundations)
- In speaker recognition the same logic is applied in operation: calibrated LLR outputs are thresholded at log(beta), where beta = (C_fa/C_miss)(1 - P_target)/P_target. [V] — [Sadjadi et al. 2022 (SRE21)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10242)
- Cost curves are an alternative to ROC curves that plot expected normalised cost against the probability-cost function. They show directly which classifier and threshold is best for each cost ratio and prior. [K] — [Drummond & Holte 2006, "Cost curves: an improved method for visualizing classifier performance", *Machine Learning* 65:95–130, doi:10.1007/s10994-006-8199-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-006-8199-5)

### Inferences
- For "miss vs. manual review": expected cost per unit time = C_FN * (missed events/hour) + C_review * (detections/hour). Here detections/hour = TP/hour + FP/hour, and it may be useful to price the review of true positives too, since every flagged item gets reviewed. Plotting this against threshold on validation data, or over a range of plausible cost ratios as with cost curves, makes the trade-off explicit and auditable. It also avoids choosing an arbitrary beta for F_beta.
- This requires calibrated scores. Uncalibrated network outputs should be recalibrated (e.g. Platt or isotonic) on held-out data, or the threshold should be chosen empirically on validation with the cost function directly.

- No source was found giving concrete cost ratios for bioacoustic or monitoring review workflows. These would have to come from the application.

## 6. Threshold selection on validation vs. test; seed variance; avoiding leakage

### Takeaway
Thresholds, post-processing parameters (e.g. median-filter lengths) and model or checkpoint selection are all hyperparameters. They must be chosen on validation data and frozen before the test set is touched. Selecting on test gives optimistically biased estimates (Cawley & Talbot; Varma & Simon). Benchmark results vary substantially with data splits, initialisation and hyperparameter sampling, so comparisons should randomise these sources and report distributions or confidence intervals (Bouthillier et al.; Dodge et al.).

### Cited Findings
- Bouthillier et al. (MLSys 2021) model the full benchmarking process and find that variance from data sampling, parameter initialisation, hyperparameter choice and data augmentation noticeably affects results. Randomising more sources of variation brings an imperfect estimator closer to the ideal one at 51× lower compute cost than the ideal estimator. They study improvement-detection error rates across five deep-learning tasks. [V] — [Bouthillier et al. 2021, "Accounting for variance in machine learning benchmarks", *Proc. MLSys* 3; arXiv:2103.03098](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2021/hash/0184b0cd3cfb185989f858a1d9f5c1eb-Abstract.html)
- Dodge et al. propose reporting *expected validation performance* as a function of the hyperparameter-tuning budget, and show that conclusions about which model is better can flip depending on budget. [K] — [Dodge, Gururangan, Card, Schwartz & Smith 2019, "Show your work: improved reporting of experimental results", EMNLP-IJCNLP, doi:10.18653/v1/D19-1224](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/D19-1224)
- Over-fitting during model selection, including choosing hyperparameters on the same data used for the performance estimate, can produce bias comparable to differences between learning algorithms. [K] — [Cawley & Talbot 2010, "On over-fitting in model selection and subsequent selection bias in performance evaluation", *JMLR* 11:2079–2107](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html)
- Tuning inside cross-validation without nesting gives biased error estimates, and nested CV is recommended. [K] — [Varma & Simon 2006, "Bias in error estimation when using cross-validation for model selection", *BMC Bioinformatics* 7:91, doi:10.1186/1471-2105-7-91](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2105-7-91)
- Domain examples of correct practice: Coucke et al. tune the KWS threshold on dev and report FRR at 0.5 FAH on test [V] ([arXiv:1811.07684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.07684)). Ebbers et al. tune SED thresholds on validation and quantify the effect (F1 51.8% → 57.2% on eval) [V] ([arXiv:2201.13148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.13148)). NIST SRE separates "actual" cost at a system-chosen threshold from "minimum" (oracle) cost [V] ([arXiv:2204.10242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10242)).
- When the target is a recall level, the threshold uncertainty from finite validation positives can be controlled with high probability through order-statistic thresholding (NP umbrella algorithm) [V] ([Tong et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03109)).

### Inferences
- Recommended protocol for a recall-first detector:
  1. Split by recording, site or session, not by clip, to avoid leakage between near-duplicate segments.
  2. Select the model or checkpoint with a threshold-free criterion focused on the relevant region, such as PSDS with an appropriate e_max or high-recall pAUC, on validation.
  3. Choose the threshold on validation to reach recall >= R* with a confidence margin, or to minimise expected cost.
  4. Freeze everything and report on test: recall, FP/hour and precision at that threshold, plus the oracle (test-optimal) values to expose the selection gap.
  5. Repeat over at least 3–5 seeds, and over splits if feasible. Report mean ± s.d. or bootstrap confidence intervals.
- Bootstrap confidence intervals should resample at the recording or event level, not at the frame level, because frames within an event are correlated.
