Elephant Listening Project · FruitPunch rumble detector · run of 16 October 2026, follow-ups 20–21 October

Which training data makes the best rumble detector?

We trained the same detector 8 times, each time on a different mix of recordings, and tested every version on whole days of forest audio it had never heard. A ninth version (E2) followed on 20 October.

Answer: setup E won. It was trained on the clearing recordings plus short clips of other sounds (thunder, rain, wind, engines, voices, wings, silence), labelled as "not a rumble". Compared with FruitPunch's original mix (setup A), it makes fewer false alarms everywhere and finds almost as many rumbles.

Follow-up (20 Oct): adding the forest recordings back to E (version E2) gives a tie, not a clear win. E2 finds more rumbles but raises more false alarms, especially on the vehicle days. Details below.

Improved detector (21 Oct): building on E2, a step-by-step search over 39 trained versions found a detector that finds about 5 more rumbles in every 100 on both tests, with the same or better precision and fewer vehicle false alarms. Details below.

How to read the numbers

PrecisionOf the alarms the detector raised, how many were real rumbles. 0.81 means 81 of every 100 alarms were real.
RecallOf all the real rumbles, how many the detector found. 0.68 means it found 68 of every 100.
Overall score (F1)One number that combines the two. It is only high when both are high. Higher is better.
False alarms per hourHow often it beeps when no elephant called. Lower is better.

Every score is from whole 24-hour recordings, checked the way ELP checks detectors: an alarm counts when its box overlaps a rumble a person marked. Each setup picked its own alarm cut-off on practice days (Complex + General) before it saw the tests.

The forest data helps the detector find 3 to 5 more rumbles in every 100, but it also brings back false alarms, nearly 3 times as many on the vehicle days (about 57 vs 20 in 72 hours). The overall scores differ by about 0.01, which is within what chance can do with one training run. So neither version is clearly better. Because missing a rumble costs more than a false alarm in this study, we built the improvement work on E2.

Improving E2 step by step

Each step tried several options and kept one only if it beat the others by more than chance. Every option was trained 5 times. Options were judged on the practice days by false alarms per hour when the detector finds 80% of the rumbles, because missing rumbles matters more than extra alarms here. The tests were scored once, at the end.

E2 is shown at its 0.90 cut-off. The improved detector uses the cut-off that found 80% of rumbles on the practice days, averaged over its 5 training runs.

  1. The alarm rule mattered most.

    FruitPunch raises an alarm when the probability stays above 0.5 for 4 frames. Letting quieter but longer stretches count (above 0.2 for at least 8 smoothed frames, with gaps of up to 2 frames joined) cut false alarms by 40% at the same recall, and it lets the detector reach 91–95% of the rumbles at its lowest cut-off.

  2. Random forest audio is the most useful "not a rumble" example.

    1,000 random frames a day from the forest days cut false alarms by another 28%. Using none was the worst option of all 39 (0.40 an hour), and 2,000 was no better than 1,000.

  3. More noise clips and more negatives made it worse.

    Repeating the noise clips 2 to 20 times, or using 2 to 5 negatives per rumble, raised false alarms each time.

  4. A longer listening window barely matters, and it is cheap.

    1.92 s was best, but every window from 1.9 s to 12 s scored within chance of each other. The classifier takes under 0.2 s per 24 h of audio at any window, against 31 s for AVES, so speed is not a concern.

  5. Learning from its own false alarms did not help.

    One round made it worse (0.38 an hour), and three rounds only tied the simpler model, so it was left out.

Final model: E2 data, 1,000 random forest frames a day, noise clips once, 1 negative per rumble, 1.92 s window, no extra rounds. Caveat: on Test 2 recall lands at 0.78, just below the 80% target, because the cut-off was set on the practice days. Leave a small safety margin when setting the cut-off for use.

What we learned

  1. Teaching the detector what noise sounds like helped most.

    Adding the 254 noise clips (E vs C) cut false alarms on the practice days from 0.67 to 0.27 an hour and raised precision on every test. The clips made up only 7% of the "not a rumble" examples, so giving them a bigger share may help more.

  2. Clearing data does not hurt in forest. There is just too little forest data to replace it.

    Forest only (B) was the worst setup by far: 0.20 precision on Test 1, against 0.65 for clearing only. The forest set has 9 times fewer rumble examples than the clearing set. Clearing only (C) and clearing + forest (A) scored about the same.

  3. Adding more forest days to training did not help.

    F and H scored lower than E on Test 1 (0.67 and 0.71 vs 0.75), and G tied with E. These add only 7,000 to 14,000 forest rumble examples next to 173,000 from clearings, and FruitPunch's rule skips many of those days (13 of the 30 Complex days).

  4. FruitPunch's own test cannot tell the setups apart.

    On FruitPunch's short balanced clips, all 8 setups score 0.95–0.96, including B, which is the worst on whole days by far. Only the whole-day test shows the real differences.

  5. One swamp day carries Test 1.

    Day nn08c_20230104 holds 1,032 of Test 1's 1,577 rumbles. Without it, E's precision drops from 0.69 to 0.54 and recall from 0.83 to 0.78. On Test 2 the hardest habitat is mixed closed forest, where most setups find only 54–60% of rumbles (E: 54%).

  6. The practice days predicted the tests well.

    For A to E, the order on the practice days (E, C, A, D, B) matches the order on both tests. So picking on practice days, without peeking at the tests, worked.

What to do next

  1. Check the improved detector's alarms by hand in Raven on a few test days, especially in mixed closed forest and on vehicle days, which are still its weakest spots.
  2. Choose the cut-off for real use with a small safety margin, so recall stays at or above 80% on new sites.
  3. Bigger changes, such as a different feature model or more labelled forest days, can be judged with the same 80%-recall yardstick.
