Elephant Listening Project · research explainer · October 2026

Listening for rumbles

What the published research says about finding forest elephant calls in day-long recordings, what our current detector does, and the new model we propose to build. It's written for readers with no machine learning background. Every new term is explained where it first appears.

1 · The problem: a whisper under the forest

Forest elephants in Central Africa talk to each other mostly with rumbles: long, very deep calls lasting about 2 to 8 seconds. ELP leaves recorders strapped to trees for months, and each recorder captures 24 hours of sound a day. People cannot listen to all of it, so we need a program that finds the rumbles automatically. That program is called a detector.

HzHertz: vibrations per second, which we hear as pitch. A man's voice is around 100 Hz and a bird's whistle about 4,000 Hz. A rumble's basic pitch is only 10–30 Hz. Sound below about 20 Hz is called infrasound, and humans can barely hear it or not at all.

The best way to "see" sound is a spectrogram: a picture with time running left to right and pitch running bottom to top. Brightness shows how loud each pitch is at each moment. Below is a sketch of a 20-second stretch of forest sound, the way the detector sees it.

Three things make this hard:

  • Rumbles are rare. On a typical forest day they fill well under 1% of the audio. Most of the day is insects, rain, wind, other animals and silence.
  • Lookalikes exist. Thunder, wind gusts, aircraft and trucks all put energy at the same low pitches.
  • Distant rumbles are faint. Trees absorb sound, so a far-away rumble may be barely brighter than the background.

2 · How we measure success

A detector looks at each moment of audio and gives it a score from 0 to 1: how sure it is that a rumble is there. We then choose a threshold, a cut-off score. Anything above it becomes a detection, a box that an analyst will check in Raven. Moving the threshold trades two kinds of mistakes against each other.

RecallOf all the real rumbles, the share the detector found. Missing a rumble means losing data about an endangered animal, so for ELP recall comes first.
PrecisionOf all the detections, the share that really are rumbles. Low precision means analysts waste time on false alarms.
FA/hFalse alarms per hour of audio. This is our main yardstick, because it counts the analyst's wasted effort directly and doesn't change with how many elephants happened to be around.

Lowering the threshold finds more rumbles but lets in more noise. A better detector does not just pick a better threshold: it pushes the two colours further apart, so that one cut-off catches nearly all the amber marks and almost no rose ones. So we compare models at a fixed recall, usually 80%, and ask which one gives the fewest false alarms per hour there. Lower is better.

The trap in many published numbers

Many papers test on balanced clips: short snippets that are half rumbles and half noise. Real days are nothing like that.

3 · Machine learning, in plain words

Older detectors were written as rules by hand, for example "a rumble is a bright line between 10 and 40 Hz lasting over 2 seconds". Machine learning (ML) works the other way round. You show the computer thousands of examples marked "rumble" or "not rumble", and it works out its own rule.

LabelsThe human answers attached to training examples. ELP's analysts drew boxes around rumbles in Raven. Those boxes are our labels.
TrainingThe computer adjusts millions of internal knobs (weights) a tiny bit at a time, so its scores match the labels better. One pass through all examples is an epoch.
ModelThe learned rule: the structure (architecture) plus the trained weights. A neural network is a model made of layers. Each layer transforms the numbers from the one below into slightly more meaningful numbers.

Three ideas from modern ML carry most of the weight in this report:

Fingerprints (embeddings)

A big neural network can turn each short slice of sound into a list of 768 numbers, an embedding, which we've been calling a "fingerprint". Sounds that resemble each other get similar fingerprints. A small, simple model on top (a classifier or head) only has to learn which fingerprints mean "rumble".

Pretraining and fine-tuning

Big networks are first pretrained on huge amounts of general audio, so they already know what sound "looks like". Self-supervised pretraining needs no labels at all. The network hides pieces of the audio from itself and learns to predict them, which works like filling in missing words in a sentence. You can then:

  • freeze the network and only train a small head on its fingerprints (cheap, and what we do today), or
  • fine-tune it: let the big network itself change a little so it gets better at your sound. LoRA is a cheap way to fine-tune. Instead of changing all 95 million weights, it adds about 0.1% extra weights that learn the adjustment. Afterwards they can be merged in, so the detector runs no slower.

4 · How our detector works today

Our detector descends from FruitPunch's. It uses AVES, a transformer pretrained on many animal sounds, which is good at birds, frogs and insects. AVES has never heard infrasound, so we use a trick.

The full pipeline today:

  1. Speed up

    Resample to 500 Hz and present to AVES as 16 kHz.

  2. Fingerprint

    AVES (frozen, last layer) gives a 768-number fingerprint every 0.64 s.

  3. Classify

    A small head (an MLP, a simple network a few layers deep) looks at 17 neighbouring fingerprints (10.88 s) and scores "rumble or not". It was trained with 254 noise clips (used 10 times each), 3,000 random forest frames per day and half a frame of "gap" negatives per rumble frame.

  4. Alarm rule

    Smooth the scores over 5 frames, start a detection when they rise above 0.2, require at least 3 frames, join gaps of up to 3 frames. Each detection's score is the highest frame score inside it.

5 · What others have built

We read every published elephant detector we could find, plus the closest work on whales and birds. For forest elephants in long recordings, there are surprisingly few papers. None tests on forest-floor recorders and whole, fully checked days the way we do, so there is no published number on our task to beat.

  1. 2010

    Venter & Hanekom: pitch-based detector

    Savanna elephants. Tracks the rumble's pitch directly. Reported precision and recall around 86%.

    hand-made rulessmall data
  2. 2015

    Zeppelzauer, Hensman & Stoeger: cleaning the picture first

    Treats the spectrogram as an image and boosts line-like harmonic shapes before classifying (structure-tensor enhancement + SVM). This halved false alarms (24% → 14%) at 88% detection. Engines caused most false alarms, just like for us.

    6 h, 635 rumbles, 1 siteenhancement halves FAs
  3. 2017

    Keen, Shiu, Wrege & Rowland: ELP's first big detector

    Hand-designed "harmonic" and "horizontal energy" measurements on 0.1 s frames, scored by AdaBoost. Trained on 522 h (10,721 rumbles) and tested on 3,983 h from 5 clearings. 83% recall at about 20 false alarms per hour. Much worse at sites it had never seen.

    clearingsrealistic testingpoor on new sites
  4. 2019

    Bjorck et al. (Cornell, AAAI): first deep networks on ELP data

    DenseNet (a CNN) classifies 25-second windows; a CNN + LSTM marks where calls start and stop. Data: 4,899 h, 168k calls. 89.7% accuracy, but on balanced windows with a random split, so not comparable with real-day numbers. Adding the recurrent layer lifted segmentation from 70% to 92%.

    CNN + LSTMbalanced testsequence model helps
  5. 2020

    Stanford and Conservation Metrics detectors

    Neural networks used by ELP in Nouabalé-Ndoki. Architectures are unpublished. Stanford's scored P 0.84 / R 0.75 on Sample A in ELP's internal evaluation.

    our sitesnot published
  6. 2024

    FruitPunch AI challenge: AVES + 32× speed-up

    Frozen AVES fingerprints + small head. "High 90s" on balanced clips, 0.245 precision on whole days. This is the starting point we've already improved.

    transformer, frozenbalanced test
  7. 2024

    Geldenhuys & Niesler: "Learning to rumble"

    Compared simple models, CNNs and Audio Spectrogram Transformers (AST) on savanna and Asian elephant recordings. A transformer that outputs a score every 0.1 s won, with average precision 0.97 and 0.77. Long calls were sometimes split in two.

    handheld recordingstransformer best
  8. 2025

    Pickering et al.: bird models describe elephant calls

    Dzanga Bai calls, slowed into bird models (Perch, BirdNET) and general models (VGGish, YAMNet). All separated rumbles, roars and trumpets well (96–97% accuracy). This is classification of already-cut calls, not detection.

    time-expanded audio1,254 calls
  9. 2026

    DeepForestSound (Kibale, Uganda)

    An AST fine-tuned with LoRA, using a special low-pitch spectrogram (1 kHz, 3–250 Hz) for elephants. Elephant AP 0.961, against 0.672 for the same network frozen and 0.117 for frozen Perch 2.0. It is scored per minute, not per call, and in one forest. Code is public.

    fine-tuning wins bigone forest
  10. 2026

    Geldenhuys & Niesler: "From birdsong to rumbles"

    Tested 15+ frozen pretrained models on elephant call types. Bird models (Perch 2.0, Perch 1, BirdNET) transferred best, within about 2% of a fully trained transformer on one measure, but clearly behind on average precision. Middle layers often beat the last layer.

    frozen embeddingsmiddle layers help
APAverage precision: one number from 0 to 1 that summarises precision across all thresholds. 1 means every real call scores above every noise sound.

Asian elephant work (Sri Lanka, India, Kenya) mostly classifies short clips or builds village early-warning devices with small, balanced datasets reporting 89–99% accuracy. Those numbers don't carry over to month-long forest recordings. The full table with datasets, metrics, limitations and code links is in the written survey.

6 · Three lessons from elephants, whales and birds

Lesson 1: let the big network adapt

Lesson 2: judge each moment in its context

Fin whales sing 20 Hz notes, the nearest thing in the ocean to a rumble. Adding a recurrent network that reads about 2 minutes of the CNN's outputs at once improved everything:

A rumble has a rise and fall over several seconds, and rumbles often come in exchanges between elephants. Thunder has a sudden start, and an engine drones for minutes. A model that can see a minute or two around each moment can tell these apart. Our own rerun already points the same way: widening the head's view from 0.64 s to 10.88 s was one of the biggest single gains (validation score 0.271 → 0.220), and vehicle false alarms fell from 0.73 to 0.44 per hour. Beyond about 11 s, pasting even more fingerprints side by side stopped helping. A recurrent or attention-based head shares its weights across time and learns what to remember, which is a more efficient way to look further.

F1A single score that balances precision and recall (their harmonic mean). "Peak F1" is the best value over all thresholds.

Lesson 3: unlabelled audio is a goldmine

animal2vec pretrained a network on 1,068 hours of unlabelled meerkat recordings. The pretraining was adapted to calls that are short and rare. With only 1% of the labels it reached AP 0.83, against 0.30 for a standard speech-style network trained on all of them. In sound-event competitions (DCASE), the biggest single gain came from self-training. A first model labels unlabelled audio, its most confident guesses (pseudo-labels) become extra training data, and a second model learns from both. ELP has hundreds of thousands of unlabelled hours. At our 32× speed-up that's a huge training set, and nobody has used it this way yet.

Two smaller lessons. Whale detectors gained most from hard negatives (retraining on the detector's own false alarms), which we already do. A noise-adaptive spectrogram called PCEN made bird detection steadier as insect noise changed through the night.

7 · The proposal: a detector that listens longer and learns our forest

We propose to combine the parts that the evidence supports into one model. Each part is cheap to run, and each can be switched off for testing. No published elephant detector combines them.

A

Teach AVES about rumbles (LoRA + layer blend)

Instead of using AVES frozen, add small LoRA add-ons so it can adjust to sped-up rumbles. Read a weighted blend of its layers, because middle layers hold finer pitch detail than the last.

Why: frozen vs adapted was 0.67 vs 0.96 AP in DeepForestSound, and middle layers often beat the last in elephant tests. LoRA costs nothing extra when running, because it merges in. Our own AVES-tuning run so far is less encouraging: layer 12 beat every other layer and the learned layer mix (0.134 vs 0.150 and worse, one run each), and the first LoRA run (rank 4) ended no better than where it started. So A is now the least certain part of the proposal. The remaining LoRA and top-layer options in that run will decide whether it stays.

B

A second pair of ears tuned to low pitch

A small network that looks at the real-speed spectrogram between 5 and 250 Hz. It uses PCEN, a loudness adjustment that follows slow changes in background noise. Its convolutions know which pitch band they are looking at (frequency-dynamic convolution).

Why: engines and thunder are our worst false alarms. Clear harmonic shapes cut false alarms in half for savanna rumbles. DeepForestSound used exactly this kind of low-pitch view. Nobody has compared the "speed up" approach with the "low-pitch view" approach, or combined them. This tests and fuses both.

C

Read a minute or two, not two seconds

A bidirectional GRU (a recurrent network that reads the sequence both forwards and backwards) runs over 64–192 frames, about 40 seconds to 2 minutes. It is trained on whole 2-minute stretches, so it learns that rumbles are rare and come in bouts, and that engines drone on.

Why: in the fin-whale study, false alarms fell 59–85%. Bjorck's ELP model gained 70% → 92% from a recurrent layer, and all top sound-event systems use one. It is also cheap: we can train it in hours on the fingerprints we've already saved.

D

Learn from unlabelled forest days

Let the best model score unlabelled forest days (never test days), keep only very confident rumbles and very confident non-rumbles, and train a fresh model on labelled plus pseudo-labelled data. Repeat twice. The same model also flags likely unmarked rumbles hiding in the clearing data's "negatives" (the clearing tables say "not every rumble marked"), and we stop penalising those.

Why: in DCASE this was the largest single gain. It adds forest examples, which we lack (3,200 vs 31,000 in clearings), without anyone labelling more.

E

Optional: give AVES "forest ears"

Continue AVES's self-supervised pretraining on thousands of hours of unlabelled ELP audio, sped up 32×, with masking suited to rare calls. Then repeat A–D.

Why: in-domain pretraining was the big win for meerkats, and it generalises best to new years and sites. Cost: days to weeks of computing, so we only do this if A–D leave a clear gap on the 2023 test days.

8 · The experiment plan

We keep the rules that already work. All choices are made on the validation days, and the main measure is false alarms per hour at 80% recall (also 85% and 90%), averaged over 5 training runs. The test days are scored once at the very end. We add one part at a time and keep it only if it beats the current best by more than the run-to-run spread.

  1. Fair baselines

    Re-run our current model, a frozen Perch 2.0 probe, and DeepForestSound's public model, all on our data, scoring and alarm-rule tuning. Then "beats other models" is measured, not claimed.

    About 1–2 days

  2. Context reader (C) on saved fingerprints

    Compare a BiGRU, a temporal CNN and a small Conformer, with 40 s, 80 s and 2 min windows. Hypothesis: at least 20% fewer false alarms at 80% recall, and higher maximum recall.

    Hours, with no new AVES runs

  3. Layer blend (A, part 1): already tested

    The AVES-tuning run found layer 12 alone best; a learned mix of all layers was slightly worse. Retry the mix only under the context reader, where finer detail might matter more.

    Mostly done

  4. Adapted AVES + context reader (A + C)

    Only if the AVES-tuning run finds a LoRA or top-layer option that beats frozen layer 12: train it together with the context reader.

    1–3 h per run × 5 runs

  5. Low-pitch branch (B)

    Add the 5–250 Hz branch. Check plain log spectrogram vs PCEN, and three noise-tracking speeds. Watch vehicle-day false alarms in particular.

    Hours

  6. Self-training (D)

    Two rounds of teacher → pseudo-labels → student on unlabelled forest days. Stop if the validation days get worse.

    About a day per round

  7. Second-look checker and better boxes

    A small model re-checks each candidate using 10 s around it. A better method for placing start and end times (SEBB) is compared with our tuned alarm rule.

    Hours

  8. Optional: forest pretraining (E)

    Only if the 2023 test (Sample B) still lags clearly behind Sample A.

    Days to weeks; a rented GPU helps

  9. Final

    Combine the best 3–5 runs into an ensemble (average their scores). Check CPU speed against ELP's target (4,500 recording-days on 30 cores in under 5 days). Score the sealed tests once.

    Hours

What "winning" means.
  • On ELP's 42-day Sample A, scored exactly as ELP scored Stanford: precision above 0.842 at recall of at least 0.746 (our current model already looks ahead on 54 days, so confirm it on the same 42).
  • Fewer false alarms per hour at 80% and 90% recall than our current model, Perch 2.0 and DeepForestSound on the validation days and the 2023 test days.
  • All measured as averages over training runs.

9 · What could go wrong

  • A longer context might add little beyond 11 s. Our wider-window results flattened out after about 11 s. The whale result was on top of a CNN, which sees much less context than AVES. That's why the context reader is tested first: it is the cheapest step.
  • Adapting AVES may not pay off on our data. In the tuning run so far, layer 12 frozen is still best and the first LoRA run didn't improve. If the remaining options agree, we drop part A and put the effort into C, B and D.
  • Fine-tuning can over-learn the clearings. 90% of rumble examples come from three clearing sites. We keep balanced sampling and stop training based on held-out forest days.
  • Self-training can amplify mistakes. A bioacoustics study found some adaptation methods did worse than doing nothing. We use strict confidence cut-offs and stop if validation gets worse.
  • PCEN can erase rumbles if it adapts faster than a rumble lasts. We tune its speed and keep a plain spectrogram as a control.
  • The gains above are estimates drawn from other studies, not measurements on our data. The plan is built to find out cheaply.

Glossary

AdaBoost / SVM / Random forestClassic statistical models that combine hand-made measurements into a decision.
Alarm ruleOur steps that turn a curve of frame scores into detections: smoothing, an "on" level, minimum length, gap joining.
AP (average precision)Precision averaged over all thresholds. 1.0 is perfect ranking.
ASTAudio Spectrogram Transformer: a transformer that reads spectrogram patches.
AttentionThe mechanism letting a transformer weigh how much each moment matters to every other.
AVESA transformer (HuBERT design) pretrained without labels on animal sounds. Our current backbone.
Balanced clipsTest snippets that are half positives. Flatters detectors of rare sounds.
BiGRU / LSTMRecurrent networks that read sequences and remember context. "Bi" means both directions.
BirdNET / PerchLarge networks trained on labelled bird (and some other) sounds, widely reused for other animals.
CNNConvolutional neural network: scans images or spectrograms with learned pattern detectors.
DCASEAn annual competition and workshop on detecting and classifying sound events.
Embedding / fingerprintA list of numbers describing a slice of sound; similar sounds get similar lists.
EnsembleAveraging several models' scores; usually trims random mistakes.
EpochOne pass of training over all examples.
F1A combined score of precision and recall.
False alarms per hour (FA/h)Wrong detections per hour of audio. Our main yardstick, read at a fixed recall.
Fine-tuningLetting a pretrained network adjust its own weights to your task.
FrozenUsing a pretrained network without changing it; only a small head is trained.
Hard negativesNoise the detector wrongly flagged, added back as "not rumble" training examples.
HarmonicsFainter copies of a sound at 2×, 3×… its basic pitch.
Head / classifierThe small model that turns fingerprints into a rumble score.
InfrasoundSound below about 20 Hz, mostly below human hearing.
LabelsHuman-marked answers used for training and testing (Raven boxes).
LoRALow-rank adaptation: fine-tuning via small add-on weights that can be merged in later.
MLPMulti-layer perceptron: the simplest kind of neural network, a few stacked layers.
PCENPer-channel energy normalisation: a loudness adjustment per pitch band that tracks background noise.
PrecisionShare of detections that are real rumbles.
PretrainingTraining on large general data before your specific task.
Pseudo-labels / self-trainingA model's own confident guesses on unlabelled audio, reused as training data.
RecallShare of real rumbles found.
Seed / training runTraining starts from random weights; different seeds give slightly different models, so we average 5.
Self-supervised learningLearning from unlabelled data by predicting hidden parts of it.
SpectrogramA picture of sound: time across, pitch up, brightness = loudness.
ThresholdThe score cut-off that decides what becomes a detection.
TransformerA network built around attention; the basis of AVES, AST and most modern audio models.
Validation vs test daysValidation days are used to make choices. Test days are scored once at the end for an honest result.
