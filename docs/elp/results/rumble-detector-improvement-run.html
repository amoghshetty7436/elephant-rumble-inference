Elephant Listening Project · forest elephant rumble detector · 16–21 October 2026

How we made the rumble detector find more elephants

We took the rumble detector that FruitPunch AI built for ELP and tried to make it miss fewer forest elephant calls without drowning reviewers in false alarms. This page explains the whole process from the start: how the detector works, what data it learns from, how we judged each version, every option we tried, what won, and how long it all took.

Result: on recordings it had never heard, the final detector finds about 5 more rumbles in every 100 than our previous best version (E2). It raises the same number of false alarms or fewer, and it runs just as fast.

It scans a 24-hour recording in about 31 seconds on the lab PC (about 1.3 s per hour of audio). The biggest gains came from two changes: a smarter rule for turning the detector's scores into alarms, and showing it more ordinary forest background as "not a rumble". Several other ideas were tested carefully and did not help.

1 · Background

How the detector works

Forest elephants call to each other with low rumbles, mostly below 100 Hz. ELP records the forest around the clock, and the detector's job is to point people at the moments in those 24-hour recordings that contain a rumble. It works in five steps:

Input24 h of audioOne recorder, one day, as a sound file.
Step 1Cut into frames0.64 s slices, about 134,500 per day.
Step 2 · fixedAVES fingerprintA pre-trained animal-sound model turns each frame into 768 numbers. We never change it.
Step 3 · trainedSmall classifierReads the fingerprints and gives each frame a rumble probability from 0 to 1.
Step 4 · tunedAlarm ruleJoins high-probability frames into alarms and gives each a score.
OutputRaven tableOne box per alarm, ready for a person to check.

Only the highlighted steps change in this work. The classifier is small, so retraining it takes minutes. That is what made it possible to try dozens of versions in one day. The AVES fingerprints are computed once and stored on disk.

3 · Method

How we judged each version

RecallOf all the real rumbles, the share the detector found. 0.80 means it found 80 of every 100. For a species this threatened, this is the number we care about most.
PrecisionOf all the alarms it raised, the share that were real rumbles. 0.65 means 65 of every 100 alarms were real.
False alarms per hourHow often it raises an alarm when no elephant called. This is the extra checking work a person has to do.
Cut-offEach alarm has a score. Alarms below the cut-off are dropped. A lower cut-off finds more rumbles but also raises more false alarms.

The yardstick: false alarms per hour at 80% recall

Comparing two versions at the same cut-off is unfair, because one might be "stricter" than the other. So for every version we lower the cut-off until it finds 80% of the practice-day rumbles, then count how many false alarms per hour it raises at that point. The version with fewer false alarms wins. This puts recall first, as the study needs, and is how detectors are usually compared in the literature when missing calls is the bigger cost.

Each dot is one cut-off. The vertical axis is recall, not the cut-off: to move up from 80% to 90% recall the cut-off has to be lowered, which lets more false alarms through, so the line moves right. Real measurements from this run: the model after step 1 (E2's data with only the new alarm rule) and the final model. Both reach 80%, but the final model gets there with fewer false alarms, and the gap grows at higher recall. We chose 80% because most earlier versions could not reach 90% at all.

The same idea in counts

The practice days hold about 1,392 hours of audio and 1,113 real rumbles. The cut-off is the setting we choose: an alarm is kept only if its score is above it. Recall is the result we get. A strict (high) cut-off keeps only confident alarms, so it misses faint rumbles and recall is lower. A looser (lower) cut-off catches more rumbles but lets in more false alarms. So for each recall we ask for, we find the cut-off that gives it and count the false alarms.

Both catch the same rumbles, but the final model pays about 150 fewer false alarms for them. That is the yardstick: the fewer false alarms a model needs to reach 80%, the better. On the graph above, each table becomes one line, and the line further to the left is the better model.

80% is for comparing, not a limit on use. We compared models at 80% because most early versions could not reach 90%. The winner is also best at 85% and 90%. In practice the detector can run at about 90% recall, at roughly 11 false alarms per recorder-day to review instead of about 5. Its ceiling is about 91%: some rumbles are too faint or masked for it to hear at any cut-off.

Every version trained 5 timesTraining involves chance: the starting weights and the order of examples are random. So each version was trained 5 times and we report the average. Each run trains for a fixed 5 minutes (FruitPunch's method), not a fixed number of epochs, so bigger or slower training sets get fewer passes: 60–64 epochs for the 0.64 s versions, 30–35 for wider windows, and only 14–23 with 2 to 5 negatives per rumble. The range across the 5 runs (shown as a line on the charts) shows how much a score moves by luck alone.
The tie ruleIf two options differ by less than the run-to-run spread, the difference may be luck. In that case we keep the simpler, cheaper option, such as a shorter listening window or fewer extra rounds. This stops us adding complexity that doesn't clearly pay.

What I read from Part 1

  • Teaching it what noise sounds like helped most. C to E only added the 254 noise clips, yet false alarms fell on every test and vehicle false alarms dropped from 0.46 to 0.28 an hour.
  • The forest data is too small to stand alone. Forest only (B) found many rumbles (recall 0.90) but 4 of every 5 alarms were false, and it fired almost 7 times an hour on engines. With 9 times fewer rumbles than the clearing data, it cannot learn what is not a rumble.
  • Clearing data transfers well to the forest. Clearing only (C) beat the forest-heavy D on every number, so the rumble itself sounds similar enough across habitats.
  • Forest data adds recall, at the price of false alarms. When forest days were added to a setup (C to A, E to E2, E to G), recall on Test 1 rose by 0.01 to 0.04 and precision fell. That trade is why E2 suits a recall-first study.
  • FruitPunch's clip test can't rank detectors. All 9 setups scored 0.95–0.96 on it, including B, the worst by far on whole days. Whole-day scoring is necessary.
  • The practice days were a good guide. For A to E, the order on the practice days matched the order on both tests.

Decision: E and E2 tied on overall score (F1). E2 found 3 to 5 more rumbles in every 100 but raised more false alarms. Because missing elephants costs more than an extra alarm, we built Part 2 on E2, aiming to win back the false alarms without losing the recall.

5 · Part 2, 20–21 October

Improving E2 one step at a time

We changed one thing at a time, in a fixed order, so we would know what each change did. Each step tried several options, kept the best one (or the simpler one if it was a tie), and passed it on to the next step. Step 4 then retested the top two options of each earlier step together, in case some only work in combination.

The whole run was a single command left running on the lab PC. It saved every result as it went, so it could resume after an interruption without redoing anything.

    What I read from Part 2 as a whole

    • Where the alarms come from matters more than the classifier. The two alarm-rule steps gave about two thirds of the total gain, for no training cost at all. FruitPunch's rule was throwing away faint rumbles before the classifier's skill could show.
    • The kind of negative examples matters more than how many. Ordinary forest background was the most valuable. Simply adding more of anything (noise repeats, gap negatives, 2,000 random frames) made it worse every time. The balance of rumble and non-rumble examples looks close to right at 1:1.
    • The best versions sit in a tight band. The top 11 versions all sit between 0.19 and 0.23 false alarms an hour, mostly within each other's luck range. The detector seems close to what this setup (frozen AVES fingerprints plus a small classifier) can do with this data. Bigger gains will probably need a new ingredient, such as more labelled forest days or a fingerprint model tuned to low-frequency calls.
    • Wider windows help at higher recall more than at 80%. At 90% recall the 0.64 s model gives 0.53 false alarms an hour, against 0.42–0.46 for the 3.2 s to 9.6 s windows. If ELP later wants 90% recall, a longer window becomes more worth having.
    • Learning from its own mistakes was unstable. One round of hard negatives had the widest luck range of any version (0.29–0.53). One possible cause is that some mined "false alarms" were faint rumbles nobody had labelled, which would teach the model to ignore real calls. It is not worth repeating in this form.
    • Run-to-run luck is about ±0.02 to ±0.05 false alarms an hour. Any single-run comparison smaller than that, like several in Part 1, should be read as a tie.

    8 · Takeaways

    What we learned

    1. The alarm rule mattered as much as the training.

      FruitPunch's rule needs 4 frames above 0.5. Many real forest rumbles are fainter than that, so they could never be found. Letting longer stretches above 0.2 count, after light smoothing, cut false alarms by 40% and let the detector reach 91–95% of rumbles. It costs nothing at run time.

    2. Ordinary forest background is the most useful "not a rumble" example.

      Random moments from the forest days cut false alarms by another 28%. Leaving them out was the worst option of all 39 versions.

    3. More is not always better.

      Repeating the noise clips, adding more negatives per rumble, and doubling the random frames to 2,000 all made results worse. They seem to crowd out the real rumbles the detector learns from.

    4. Hearing more context helps only a little.

      A 1.92 s window was a small step up from 0.64 s. Windows up to 12 s did no better, and 8.32 s tied with 1.92 s, so the simpler one was kept.

    5. Learning from its own mistakes was not worth it here.

      One round made results clearly worse. Three rounds came back to a tie, at about four times the training cost.

    6. The practice days predicted the tests.

      Every step that clearly helped on the practice days also helped on both tests, and the small final rule change was neutral there. So the choices were not just fitted to the practice set.

    Limits to keep in mind

    • On Test 2 the detector found 78% of rumbles, just under the 80% it was tuned for. A cut-off set on one set of days usually lands a little lower on new ones, so set it with a small safety margin when using the detector.
    • Engine noise is still the main source of false alarms: about 0.64 an hour on the vehicle days.
    • Test 1 is dominated by one swamp day (nn08c_20230104), which holds about two thirds of its rumbles.
    • Training had a fixed 5-minute budget, so the versions with 2 to 5 negatives per rumble got only 14–23 epochs. They may have lost partly from being under-trained, so "more negatives hurts" is likely but not proven.
    • Each step was tuned with the earlier winners fixed. Step 4 rechecked the strongest combinations, but not every possible one.

    Next steps

    1. Listen through the final model's Raven tables for a few test days, especially in mixed closed forest and on the vehicle days.
    2. Use the final rule with a 2-frame minimum instead of 8 (on above 0.2, smoothing 5, join gaps of 2, average). It costs nothing at 80% recall and raises the most rumbles the detector can reach from 91% to 97%, which matters if ELP runs it at 90% recall.
    3. Choose the cut-off for real use with a small safety margin, so recall stays at or above 80% on new sites.
    4. Judge any bigger change, such as a different fingerprint model or more labelled forest days, with the same 80%-recall yardstick.
